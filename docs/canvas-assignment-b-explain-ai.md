# Assignment B: Explain One AI Idea to a Nontechnical Audience Age Five or Older

## Purpose

Translate one CPSC 481 knowledge point into a short, technically accurate learning experience for a precisely defined nontechnical audience. You will preserve the causal idea while changing language, examples, representation, pacing, and assessment.

This is not a contest to remove the most technical detail or make an activity look entertaining. The goal is to learn whether another person can use the idea without acquiring a misconception.

## Define the audience

“The general public” is too broad. State:

- age range;
- language needs;
- prior AI or computing experience;
- learning context;
- available time;
- relevant accessibility needs;
- what participants should be able to do afterward.

Your audience may include children, teenagers, adults, families, educators, community groups, workplace teams, or other nontechnical learners age five and older.

## Required design sequence

1. Choose one bounded course concept.
2. Write one observable learning objective using a condition, learner action, and success evidence.
3. Identify prerequisite ideas and two likely misconceptions.
4. Write the technical **causal spine** that must remain true.
5. Create:
   - a plain-language explanation;
   - a concrete example or demonstration;
   - one meaning-matched visual, physical, or interactive representation;
   - an analogy with explicit mappings;
   - a sentence explaining where the analogy stops working;
   - a short prediction, sorting, drawing, explanation, or teach-back assessment.
6. Check accessibility, privacy, physical safety, emotional safety, and equivalent response modes.
7. Pilot the experience with at least two people who approximate the audience or with peers using instructor-supplied audience profiles.
8. Record observable responses, not only whether participants said they liked it.
9. Revise:
   - one explanation choice;
   - one visual or activity choice;
   - one assessment choice.
10. Explain how the public-facing experience connects back to the college-level technical account.

## Suggested concept families

- training examples, features, labels, and generalization;
- classification and decision boundaries;
- probability or confidence versus certainty;
- reward and trial-and-error learning;
- tokens and next-token prediction;
- retrieval and evidence-grounded answers;
- tool use, permission, and human oversight.

## Deliverables

- Audience profile and one observable learning objective.
- 5–8 minute teaching artifact or activity.
- Facilitation guide with materials, timing, questions, access, privacy, and safety notes.
- Plain-language explanation and technical causal spine.
- Analogy map with at least three correspondences and one explicit break point.
- Meaning-matched visual, physical, or interactive representation.
- Before/after misconception check or teach-back evidence.
- Pilot observation record for two participants or role profiles.
- Revision log linking observations to one explanation, one representation/activity, and one assessment revision.
- 300–500 word technical-fidelity statement explaining what you simplified, what you retained, and where the analogy stops.

## Pilot evidence template

For each participant or role profile, record:

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Prompt or task | The exact prediction, sorting, drawing, explanation, or teach-back request. |
| Observable response | What the participant said, selected, drew, ordered, or did. |
| Causal link demonstrated | Which part of the learning objective the response supports. |
| Misconception or uncertainty | The exact response that revealed a gap; do not infer a diagnosis. |
| Design revision | The explanation, representation/activity, or assessment change justified by the observation. |

“They liked it,” “it was fun,” or “they understood” is not sufficient learning evidence.

## Submission checklist

- [ ] My audience profile is specific enough to change the design.
- [ ] My objective describes an observable performance.
- [ ] I can state the technical causal spine in college-level language.
- [ ] My analogy maps structure and states where it breaks.
- [ ] My visual or activity represents the mechanism rather than decorating a text box.
- [ ] My assessment requires the learner to do something that demonstrates the objective.
- [ ] I planned equivalent access and response modes.
- [ ] I used invented or de-identified examples and collected no unnecessary sensitive information.
- [ ] I recorded observed pilot responses.
- [ ] Each revision is linked to evidence.

## Rubric: 100 points

| Criterion | Points | Evidence of success |
|---|---:|---|
| Technical fidelity and boundary statements | 25 | The causal mechanism remains correct; simplifications and analogy limits are explicit. |
| Audience fit and accessibility | 20 | Language, pacing, examples, media, and response modes follow the defined profile. |
| Example, visual, and interaction quality | 15 | Representations are meaning-matched and let participants act on the idea. |
| Assessment validity | 15 | The participant response supplies observable evidence for the stated objective. |
| Evidence-based revision | 15 | Pilot observations justify specific revisions to explanation, representation/activity, and assessment. |
| Facilitation clarity, privacy, and safety | 10 | Another facilitator can run the experience responsibly from the guide. |

## Publication and grading boundary

Your course grade is based only on this rubric. Public use or publication is not required.

If you grant permission after grading, the instructor may route the artifact to a separate instructor/AAB review process. Any public use requires technical-fidelity review, accessibility and privacy review, source checking, and an authorized editorial decision.
