# Meetings 25–26: AAB evidence and public-explanation assignment briefs

This is the instructor implementation brief for the two required AAB-connected assignments. It keeps AAB participation concentrated in Week 13 while giving CPSC 481 students both research practice and experience explaining AI accurately to nontechnical audiences age five and older.

## Shared guardrail

Student work is a **candidate contribution**, not an automatically published AAB record. A submission enters an instructor/AAB review queue. Publication requires source verification, privacy review, removal of unsupported claims, and an authorized editorial decision. A registry record is descriptive evidence, not AAB endorsement of a tool, provider, curriculum, or result.

## Assignment A: AAB research evidence brief or evidence-card review

### Purpose

Turn one peer-reviewed AI-literacy or AI-education paper into traceable, cautious evidence that could help AAB compare cases, identify gaps, or improve a registry record.

### Student pathway

Students choose one:

1. **New evidence brief:** locate a relevant peer-reviewed paper that is not already represented adequately in the AAB registry.
2. **Evidence-card review:** select an existing AAB case record, obtain its primary paper, and audit the card against the paper.

### Required workflow

1. Frame a bounded evidence question: population, intervention or system, context, outcome, and study type.
2. Search scholarly sources and document the query, database, date, and inclusion decision.
3. Verify bibliographic identity with title, authors, venue, year, DOI or stable URL, and paper access.
4. Read beyond the abstract: participants, design, comparison, measures, analysis, uncertainty, results, limitations, and transfer conditions.
5. Build a claim-to-source trace with page, section, table, or figure locations.
6. Separate:
   - what the authors report;
   - the student reviewer’s interpretation;
   - missing or unclear evidence;
   - claims the design does not support.
7. Map relevant AAB fields: learner profile, setting, educational intent, activity or intervention, outcomes, challenges, safeguards, evidence type, transferability, cost/operations when reported, and extraction confidence.
8. Conduct a second-person reproducibility check: can a peer recover each claim from the cited source location?
9. Submit the evidence brief or review report plus the trace and a proposed disposition: accept, revise, hold for verification, or reject.

### Minimum deliverables

- 800–1,200 word evidence brief or card-review report.
- Stable primary-source citation and accessible paper copy or link.
- Claim-to-source matrix with at least six material claims.
- Completed AAB candidate field map.
- Limitations and missing-information section.
- 150-word editorial note describing what AAB could use and what still requires verification.

### Rubric, 100 points

- Source relevance and bibliographic verification: 15
- Study-design and evidence reasoning: 20
- Claim-to-source trace accuracy: 25
- AAB field mapping and transferability judgment: 15
- Limitations, uncertainty, and non-endorsement language: 15
- Clarity, citation practice, and reproducibility: 10

### Current AAB examples

- AAB’s public workflow is collect → review → synthesize → publish.
- AAB case records expose structured fields, source-publication information, transferability, missing information, extraction confidence, and explicit non-endorsement language.

## Assignment B: Explain AI to a nontechnical audience age five or older

### Purpose

Translate one CPSC 481 knowledge point into a technically accurate, audience-tested learning experience. The task is not “make it cute” or “remove all detail”; it is to preserve the causal idea while changing language, representation, examples, and assessment for a defined audience.

### Required audience statement

Specify age range, language needs, prior AI experience, context, available time, accessibility needs, and what the audience should be able to do afterward. “General public” is too broad.

### Required learning-design sequence

1. Choose one bounded concept from the course.
2. Write one observable learning objective.
3. Identify prerequisite ideas and two likely misconceptions.
4. Create:
   - a plain-language explanation;
   - a concrete example or demonstration;
   - one visual, physical, or interactive representation;
   - an analogy plus an explicit statement of where it breaks;
   - a short prediction, sorting, drawing, explanation, or teach-back assessment.
5. Pilot the activity with at least two people who approximate the intended audience or with peers role-playing from supplied audience profiles.
6. Record observed responses, not only whether participants said they liked it.
7. Revise one explanation choice, one visual or activity choice, and one assessment choice using the evidence.
8. Submit a facilitator guide and a short reflection connecting the public explanation back to the college-level technical account.

### Minimum deliverables

- Audience profile and observable objective.
- 5–8 minute teaching artifact or activity.
- Facilitation guide with materials, timing, questions, accessibility, privacy, and safety notes.
- Before/after misconception check or teach-back evidence.
- Revision log with observed evidence.
- 300–500 word technical fidelity statement: what was simplified, what was retained, and where the analogy stops.

### Rubric, 100 points

- Technical fidelity and boundary statements: 25
- Audience fit and accessibility: 20
- Example, visual, and interaction quality: 15
- Assessment validity: 15
- Evidence-based revision: 15
- Facilitation clarity, privacy, and safety: 10

### Suggested concept families

- training examples, features, labels, and generalization;
- classification and decision boundaries;
- probability and confidence versus certainty;
- reward and trial-and-error learning;
- tokens and next-token prediction;
- retrieval and evidence-grounded answers;
- tool use, permission, and human oversight.
